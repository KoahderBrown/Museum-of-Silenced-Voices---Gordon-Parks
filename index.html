Gordon Parks's Exhibit

The Invisible Become Visible

Exhibit Introduction

Gordon Parks (1912-2006) was born in Fort Scott, Kansas. After high school, Parks began teaching himself how to create art, specifically, he focused on how to become a better filmmaker, photographer, writer, and composer. His life’s work was focused around capturing the African American experience. Like Zora Neale Hurston, Gordon Park set out to capture the poverty, racism, and lack of representation that Black people faced. This exhibit will explore how Park used each of these means to fight against Black oppression.

ARTIFACT 01

American Gothic

Photograph (1942) - Taken in Washington, DC

The photograph above shows Ella Watson, a cleaning lady for a security administration building, standing in front of an American flag with a broom and mop. Parks created this image after hearing about Watson’s struggles with trying to feed her family despite having multiple jobs. Park also wanted to challenge Grant Wood’s famous painting “American Gothic” by replacing a White family with a Black worker, resisting the stereotype that black workers are seen as invisible labor. Additionally, Park’s goal was to give more voice to struggles in the Black labor community.

ARTIFACT 02

Red Jackson, Harlem Gang Leader

Photograph (1948) - Taken in Harlem, New York

The portrait above is of Red Jackson, a Harlem gang leader expressing peace rather than violence. Park’s goal of this photograph was to show gang members showing curiosity rather than judgment or fear. At this time, young Black men in urban areas were seen as criminals and threats, so by taking these pictures, Park humanized them showing their real personalities.

ARTIFACT 03

Emerging Man

Photograph (1952) - Taken in Harlem, New York

This image shows a Black man emerging from a manhole, pulling himself up from the darkness. Parks created this image to show how Black people have struggled for equality and visibility. By showing someone slightly coming out of a place of hiding, Parks expresses his thoughts around oppression and representation of Black people. Parks resists the stereotype of Black voices being unheard/not important by capturing a moment of emergence.

ARTIFACT 04

Shaft Trailer (1971)

Trailer of the Movie Shaft - A film directed by Gordon Parks

In 1971, Gordon Parks became the first African American to direct a major Hollywood studio film (Shaft). The film tells the story of John Shaft, a private detective, who is hired by a Harlem crime boss to rescue his daughter.

During the making of this movie, Black actors were often supporting roles or villains, so by making the main character a Black man, Parks actively resisted the film industry. Like Hurston’s main character Janie, John Shaft has self-determination and refuses to be defined by white expectations, making the film an important step in the fight for Black representation.
